A battery meter for the headset, and the mouse that cannot be read

Nov 25, 2026·
  • windows
  • bluetooth
  • season-2

This one started from a screenshot. I had boxed the corner of the taskbar and asked: what if the tray showed the battery of my headphones and my mouse? A small thing, but a dead battery in the middle of a game or a call is a real annoyance.

Before building anything I checked what my own PC could actually tell me. Windows keeps a battery percentage for Bluetooth devices that report one, the same number Settings shows. Reading it takes no driver and no admin rights. On my machine exactly one device had a reading, a sleep headset at 100%. Four other paired devices showed nothing, and Settings explained why: they were not connected. And the mouse showed nothing at all, because it is not Bluetooth. It runs on a 2.4 GHz USB dongle, and a dongle's battery is each brand's own business. I told the app it should say so honestly: "not connected" and "no battery reported" are different answers, and neither is 0%.

The first version got it wrong in a way I only caught by reading the output. It reported 100% next to the name "Microsoft Bluetooth Enumerator", not the headset. The battery lives on a service node, and I had assumed that node's parent was the device. It is not; the headset's nodes are siblings. The fix was to match them by the device's own 12-digit address. It now lists the headset at 100%, the same as Settings, and the other four as not connected.

The app is one C# file with no SDK needed: it is built by the compiler that already ships with Windows. It shows the lowest battery as a number on a green, amber or red tile, warns once when a device drops to 40% or below, re-arms after it charges past 45%, and starts with Windows only if you tick that yourself. It is open source on GitHub: coolboy450/bt-battery-tray, and GitHub's own build of it passes.

What I checked: the number matches Settings on one real device, the app stays running, a second copy closes itself, and the build works on a clean Windows machine. What I have not checked: I have not yet looked at the icon in the tray on a real screen, where a three-digit "100" may be too small at 16 pixels; I have not seen the low-battery warning appear; and Bluetooth LE devices are supported in the code but never run against real hardware. The mouse, as said, cannot be read, and I am not going to guess a vendor protocol for it.
